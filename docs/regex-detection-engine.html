0. The claim this document defends

ScamShield promised detection without any trained model: no cloud AI, no downloaded weights, no training corpus, nothing that has to phone home. What replaces the model is a table of regular expressions — written patterns that describe how Tanzanian SMS scams are actually worded — combined with a weighted scoring stage that turns the matches into a bounded 0–100 risk score.

This document exists so that any member of the team can answer three questions without notes:

  1. How does it work? Sections 1–6.
  2. How well does it work, and how do you know? Section 7.
  3. Why not use a trained AI model, and what did you give up? Sections 8–10.
Credibility rule carried over from the Inside-Out guide: every number in this document was produced by code in the repository and can be reproduced with gradlew :app:testDebugUnitTest. Where a figure comes from published literature about other systems, it is labelled as such and never presented as our own measurement.

1. Why regular expressions are the right tool here

A regular expression is a compact way of describing a set of strings. Instead of asking "does this message contain the word pin", it lets us ask far more precise questions, such as "does a requesting verb appear within 45 characters of a secret-code word, in either order, without a negation in front of it". That single question is the difference between catching a thief and accusing a bank.

Scam SMS in Tanzania is unusually well suited to this, for one reason: scam messages are scripts. The same prize text, the same "akaunti yako imefungwa", the same healer advertisement circulate almost verbatim for months, because they were written once and are re-sent by the thousand. Formulaic language is exactly what a pattern language describes well.

What a regex gives us

  • Word boundaries: tuma matches "tuma pesa" but not "nimetuma"
  • Proximity: [^.!?]{0,45} keeps two ideas inside one sentence
  • Alternation: one rule covers Swahili and English wording
  • Optionality: m-?pesa covers "mpesa" and "m-pesa"
  • Negative lookbehind: excludes "do not share your PIN"
  • Character classes: recognises links, amounts, phone numbers by shape, not by word

What a regex cannot give us

  • Meaning. "Mama yangu ni mgonjwa, naomba unisaidie" has no scam vocabulary at all
  • Generalisation. A synonym nobody wrote a rule for is invisible
  • Ranking by likelihood — that is why a scoring stage sits on top
  • Learning from the user's corrections

2. The pipeline, end to end

message + sender
↓
1. NORMALISE — Unicode NFKD, strip accents & invisible marks, lowercase, collapse spacing
↓
2. CANONICALISE — fold look-alike characters, rejoin separated letters (anti-evasion)
↓
3. MATCH — 22 compiled rules, each against the view of the text it was written for
↓
4. SCORE — strong weights + capped supporting weights − genuine-traffic weights + 5 co-occurrence bonuses
↓
5. CLASSIFY — 0–29 SAFE · 30–59 SUSPICIOUS · 60–100 SCAM, returned with the evidence

Every stage is pure and deterministic: the same message and sender always produce the same score, on any device, offline, with no state carried between messages. That property is what makes the system defensible — a supervisor can hand-compute any verdict in this document.

Four views of one message

A single message is prepared in four forms, because different rules need different things. A URL destroyed by lower-casing and character folding cannot be inspected, and capitalisation is invisible after normalisation.

ViewWhat it isRules that read it
RAWThe message exactly as receivedShouting detection (capitals survive here only)
MESSAGENormalised: folded case, accents and spacing, punctuation and digits intactLinks, domains, amounts, phone numbers, receipt markers
CANONICALNormalised and de-obfuscatedAll wording rules (credential, money, prize, threat, healer…)
SENDERNormalised originating address or sender IDKnown scam numbers, registered sender IDs

3. Stage 1 and 2 — making evasion pointless

Plain keyword matching dies the moment a scammer writes P1N instead of PIN. Because our rules are written once and used everywhere, it is far cheaper to normalise the input than to write every spelling into every rule. Two stages do this.

Stage 2 — Canonicalisation (the anti-evasion stage)

Two tricks defeat naive keyword matching, and both are undone here with regular expressions.

a) Look-alike characters

LOOKALIKE = (?<=\p{L})[0134579@$|] | [0134579@$|](?=\p{L})
0→o  1→i  3→e  4→a  5→s  7→t  9→g  @→a  $→s  |→i

The two lookarounds are the whole trick: a digit is only folded into a letter when a letter is immediately beside it. So p1n becomes pin, but the amount 50000 and the number 0754321987 are left completely alone, because they are surrounded by digits and spaces.

b) Separators pushed between letters

SPACED_RUN = \b[\p{L}0134579](?:[\s._*+-][\p{L}0134579]){2,}\b

This matches a run of at least three single characters joined by single separators — p.i.n, o-t-p, t u m a — and deletes the separators inside the run. Ordinary words are untouched, because their letters are not separated. Crucially the join runs before whitespace is collapsed, so "t u m a  p e s a" (double space between words) rejoins as two words, tuma pesa, not one.

Folding runs before and after the join, because the tricks are combined in practice: 0tp needs the fold first, p 1 n needs the join first.

Honest limit: canonicalisation reverses the tricks we anticipated. A scammer who writes peesaa, uses Cyrillic р in place of Latin p, or invents fresh slang defeats it until a fold or a rule is added. This is the structural weakness of pattern matching and section 8 does not pretend otherwise.

Anatomy of one rule — read this one aloud in the defense

R04, the money-request rule, is the one to be able to dissect on demand:

[^.!?]{0,45}
\b(?:pesa|fedha|hela|shilingi|tsh|tzs|m-?pesa|tigopesa|mixx|airtel\s?money

FragmentMeaningWhy it matters here
\bWord boundaryStops tuma from matching inside nimetuma — "I have sent the rent money" is not a request. This single character removes a whole class of false alarms.
(?: … )Non-capturing groupGroups the alternatives without the cost of capturing text we never read.
|AlternationOne rule covers Swahili and English, so the two languages cannot drift apart.
[^.!?]{0,45}Up to 45 characters, none of which ends a sentenceThe proximity test. The verb and the money word must be in the same sentence and close together, otherwise "…lipa. Kesho tutaonana. Nina pesa" would match.
m-?\s?pesaOptional hyphen / spaceCovers "mpesa", "m-pesa", "airtel money" and "airtelmoney" without four separate rules.
…reversed branchSecond alternativeSwahili word order varies: "nitumie pesa" and "pesa unitumie" are both requests.

Two other constructs are worth being able to explain:

  • Negative lookbehind in R02: (?<!\bdo not )(?<!\bnever )… — "the verb must not be preceded by a negation". This is why "Do not share your OTP with anyone" from a real bank does not fire the credential rule, while "Share your OTP now" does.
  • Lookahead / lookbehind in the character folder: (?<=\p{L})[0134579] — "a digit that has a letter immediately before it". Lookarounds match a position, not text, so the surrounding letter is not consumed and the next match can still see it.

5. Stage 4 — the scoring arithmetic

Matching alone would be a blunt instrument: one keyword would convict, and five weak coincidences would convict just as loudly as one confession. Four mechanisms shape the raw matches into a score.

MechanismRulePurpose
Strong weightsAny rule above 15 adds its full weightA direct request for a PIN or a transfer is evidence on its own
Capped supporting weightsRules of 15 or less share a budget of 30 in totalA link + an amount + a phone number + a deadline + a brand name is 64 raw. Capped at 30, that pile can raise a warning but can never reach the scam threshold by itself. This is the single most important false-positive control.
Negative rulesS01/S02/S03 subtract; the score floors at 0Receipts, security advice and registered sender IDs are how genuine traffic looks
Suppression capWhen any strong rule fired, suppression may cancel at most the supporting scoreScammers paste the bank's own "hatutakuomba PIN" under their phishing link. That copied sentence can cancel noise, never a direct ask.
Co-occurrence bonuses+20 (C05: +10) when two rules that are innocent apart fire togetherExpresses what no single keyword can: a prize and a payment; a credential request and a link; an account threat and a credential request; a bank's name and an ordinary mobile sender; a deadline and a link
score = clamp( strong + min(weak, 30) + context − suppression , 0 , 100 )

The three combination cases worth quoting

Advice that cannot hide a request

"Akaunti yako imefungwa, thibitisha PIN hapa http://bit.ly/x. Hatutakuomba PIN yako."

S01 fires — the scammer copied the bank's warning — but three strong rules fired too, so suppression is capped at the supporting score. Verdict: SCAM 100.

The same sentence, genuinely

"Your OTP is 483920. Do not share it with anyone." from CRDB.

The negative lookbehind blocks the credential rule, S01 and S03 subtract, nothing strong fires. Verdict: SAFE 0.

6. Stage 5 — verdict and explanation

ScoreClassificationWhat the app does
0–29SAFENothing is stored, nothing is shown
30–59SUSPICIOUSWarning notification, stored in history, labelled with the strongest category
60–100SCAMHigh-priority alert, stored, category named as a scam type

The verdict is named after the heaviest specific category. Generic signals (links, urgency, brand mentions) support a verdict but never name it, so a message is only called "suspicious in general" when nothing specific was found.

Every fired rule is returned as an indicator carrying its id, its signed weight, its explanation and the exact text that fired it:

R02_CREDENTIAL_REQUEST (+35): Asks the recipient to hand over a PIN, OTP or password - "thibitisha pin"
R11_UNSAFE_LINK (+30): Hides its destination behind a shortener, raw IP address or throwaway domain - "bit.ly"
C02_CREDENTIAL_LINK (+20): A credential request is paired with a web link
This is the feature no model gives you for free. The user is not told "our AI thinks this is 87% scam". They are told which words did it. In a defense, quoting the substring that fired the rule is the strongest possible answer to "how do you know?".

State this limitation before anyone else does. That corpus is a development set: the rules and the messages were written by the same people, so 1.000 measures internal consistency, not field accuracy. Its real job is regression control — every rule is global, so one edit can break twenty other messages, and this test catches that in seconds. To claim field accuracy you need messages collected from real handsets and labelled by someone who did not write the rules.

8. Comparison with a trained AI model

The honest comparison is not "rules are better". It is that the two approaches fail in opposite directions, and that one of them requires an asset we do not have: a large corpus of labelled Tanzanian SMS.

What the literature reports for trained models — and how to quote it

The benchmark most often cited in SMS work is the UCI SMS Spam Collection: about 5,570 English messages, roughly 13% spam. On it, Multinomial Naive Bayes and linear SVM over TF-IDF features are commonly reported in the literature at around 97–98% accuracy, and fine-tuned transformer models at the top of that range. Those are other people's results on another dataset, quoted here for orientation only.

The sentence to say in the defense: "Published SMS-spam classifiers report 97–98% accuracy, but on a decade-old English corpus, tested on messages drawn from the same pool they were trained on. Our traffic is Swahili–English code-mixed Tanzanian SMS, for which no comparable labelled corpus exists. Reporting their number as ours would be dishonest; what we can report is 27 passing tests, 40 labelled messages separated with no false alarms, and a verdict that names the exact words that produced it."

How effective will the regex engine actually be?

Split the answer in two, because the two halves behave very differently.

Recall — will it catch scams?

High on the families it encodes: prize/lottery, credential phishing, account threats, money requests, advance fees, fake loans, fake jobs, healer adverts. These scripts are formulaic and repeat almost word for word, which is exactly what patterns capture, and the canonicalisation stage removes the cheap evasions.

Low outside them. A scam written in fresh wording with no listed keyword — a fabricated family emergency, a new investment pitch — scores zero. A trained model has a genuine chance there; we do not. Recall is bounded by the lexicon, and the lexicon only grows when a human adds to it.

Precision — will it cry wolf?

This is the half we control well, and it is the half that decides whether the app gets uninstalled. Three mechanisms do the work: the supporting-signal cap (a pile of weak coincidences cannot convict), the three negative rules (receipts, security advice, registered sender IDs), and the negation lookbehind. On the twenty genuine messages — receipts, OTP notices, bank advisories, school-fee and family texts — there were no false alarms, and for each one we can name the mechanism that prevented it rather than just the outcome.

The path if labelled data ever arrives

The engine is already the right shape for it. Each rule produces a binary feature and the app already collects user reports. With a few thousand labelled local messages, the natural next step is not to throw the rules away but to feed them in as features to a small on-device logistic regression, letting the data set the weights we currently set by hand. That keeps the explanation ("these rules fired") while gaining calibrated weights, and it is the standard hybrid used in production spam filtering.

9. Limitations, stated plainly

  • No semantics. A scam with no listed keyword scores zero. This is structural, not a bug to be fixed by more rules.
  • The lexicon is Swahili and English only, and only the words we wrote. Sheng and regional slang are not covered.
  • Weights are hand-set by judgement, not fitted to data. They are defensible and documented, but they are not optimal.
  • The known-number list is three hard-coded numbers; scam numbers rotate constantly. It demonstrates the mechanism rather than providing real coverage.
  • Alphanumeric sender IDs can be spoofed in some networks, so S03 lowers the score for a sender that is not always trustworthy — which is why suppression can never cancel a strong rule.
  • Every rule is global. Editing one pattern changes every verdict the app will ever make; the corpus test exists precisely because of that.
  • The corpus is a development set. Field accuracy is unmeasured, and section 7 says so.

10. Questions to expect, and the answers

QuestionAnswer
"Isn't this just keyword matching?"No. Keyword matching asks whether a word is present. This asks whether a requesting verb sits within 45 characters of a secret-code word, in either order, not preceded by a negation, after undoing character substitution — and then weighs that against signals that only genuine traffic carries. R02 alone would be a keyword; the pipeline is not.
"Why not use AI?"Three reasons, in order. There is no labelled corpus of Tanzanian scam SMS to train on. SMS is private data, and an on-device rule table needs no corpus to be collected, stored or shipped. And the app has to explain itself to the user — we show the words that fired, which a model cannot do without extra machinery. We also state where a model would beat us: unseen phrasing.
"How accurate is it?"On our 40-message labelled corpus, no misses and no false alarms — but that is a development set we wrote ourselves, so it proves regression control, not field accuracy. Field accuracy requires messages we did not write, labelled by someone else.
"What happens if a scammer writes P1N?"Stage 2 folds it back to "pin" before any rule runs, because a digit is only folded when a letter is immediately beside it. The demo case is in the unit tests. What defeats us is a trick we have not anticipated, such as a Cyrillic look-alike letter.
"Won't it flag every message that says PIN?"No. A bare mention is worth 12 out of 100 — it cannot even raise a warning. It takes a request, or a combination, to convict. And "do not share your PIN" is excluded by a negative lookbehind, then reduced further by the security-advice rule.
"Could a regex hang the app?"Every quantifier is bounded, there is no nested unbounded quantifier over an alternation, patterns are compiled once, and SMS input is short. Measured worst case is 0.12 ms per message.
"How do you add a new scam?"One row in the rule table: id, pattern, weight, explanation, category, view. Then run the corpus test to confirm the other forty messages did not move.
"Why 30 and 60 as thresholds?"They are the only tuning knobs and they are chosen so that one strong rule (25–35) raises a warning but does not convict, while a strong rule plus its combination bonus does. A test asserts every corpus scam clears 30 and that most clear 60.
